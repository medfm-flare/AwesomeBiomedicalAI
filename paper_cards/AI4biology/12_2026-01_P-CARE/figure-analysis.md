# Figure Analysis: Genomic risk model to implement precision prostate cancer screening in clinical care: the ProGRESS study

Analysis of the three main figures, read from their published captions in the
open-access full text. **No figure image is reproduced.** The captions carry the
argument; the images are the publisher's and are not redistributed here. Open
them at the [article](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43018-025-01103-0) or on
[PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13181739/) alongside this file.

This paper is unusual for the catalogue in that its figures are thin and its
**tables** carry the evidence. Fig. 1 is a workflow schematic, Fig. 2 is a
three-bar comparison and Fig. 3 is a set of incidence curves; every hazard ratio,
every risk-category proportion and every absolute cumulative incidence lives in
Tables 1–4. A figure-only reading would come away with the pipeline and none of
the numbers, so the tables are analysed here as exhibits in their own right.

## Fig. 1 — The discovery-to-implementation workflow

- **Argumentative role:** Orientation, and the paper's actual thesis statement. The seven numbered steps *are* the contribution: train PHS601 in MVP, build P-CARE from it, replicate in PRACTICAL, validate the BGE platform for P-CARE against WGS and in All of Us, validate BGE for rare-variant panel analysis, build the clinical reports, and deploy the pipeline in a randomized trial. Nothing in the figure is a result; it is a map of a translation chain.
- **Panel logic:** A single linear sequence rather than panels. Steps 1–3 are statistics, steps 4–5 are laboratory validation, steps 6–7 are clinical artefacts. The break between step 3 and step 4 is where most polygenic-score papers stop, and the figure is drawn precisely to make that break visible.
- **Reusable design:** When the contribution is a pipeline rather than a method, put the pipeline first and number the steps, so a reader can locate every later result on it. Each of the paper's five result subsections maps onto a numbered step here, which makes the Results section navigable without a second reading.
- **Boundary:** A workflow diagram asserts that the steps were completed, not that any of them succeeded well. Step 7 in particular shows the trial as an endpoint of the diagram, but ProGRESS reports no outcomes in this paper — 5,000 VA patients are randomized to usual care versus P-CARE-informed screening and the comparison is still running. The figure also does not show the failure modes that the Results do report: PMS2 undercoverage and three of six validation CNVs falling below clinical reporting quality.
- **Locator:** [Paper: Fig. 1]

## Fig. 2 — Positive predictive value of PSA within P-CARE strata

- **Argumentative role:** The one exhibit that shows P-CARE improving an existing clinical test rather than merely ranking men. It is also the only main figure whose numbers are external to MVP.
- **Panel logic:** Three bars, one comparison: mean PPV with 95% CI of a PSA ≥ 3 ng/mL result for clinically significant prostate cancer among all men in ProtecT (n = 6,411), among the top 20% of P-CARE values, and among the top 5%. The comparator is not another model — it is the same PSA threshold applied to an unstratified population. PPV rises from 0.13 (0.12–0.14) to 0.19 (0.16–0.21) to 0.23 (0.17–0.28).
- **Reusable design:** Express the model's benefit as a change in the operating characteristics of the test clinicians already order. "HR per s.d. of 2.04" is not actionable at the bedside; "the same PSA result means roughly twice as much in these men" is. Choosing ProtecT for this analysis is the other half of the design — it is the only dataset here with systematic biopsy in both cases and controls, so PPV is estimable without verification bias.
- **Boundary:** The gain is real but small in absolute terms, and the confidence intervals overlap: 0.19 (0.16–0.21) versus 0.23 (0.17–0.28) does not separate the top 20% from the top 5%. Even at the top 5%, **roughly three in four positive PSA results are still not clinically significant cancer**. The figure gives PPV only — no negative predictive value, no biopsies-avoided-per-cancer-detected, and therefore no net-benefit statement. ProtecT is a UK population-based screening trial, so the stratum PPVs are not automatically the VA's. The PSA-level-stratified version and the true-positive-capture view are pushed to Extended Data Figs. 1 and 2.
- **Locator:** [Paper: Fig. 2; Extended Data Figs. 1, 2]

## Fig. 3 — Cause-specific cumulative incidence in MVP by P-CARE strata

- **Argumentative role:** Converts hazard ratios into absolute, age-indexed risk — the form a screening conversation actually needs. This is the figure behind the "risk-equivalent age" argument.
- **Panel logic:** A 3 × 2 grid. Rows are the endpoints (A: any prostate cancer, B: metastatic, C: fatal). The left column stratifies by P-CARE percentile group (0–20th, 30–70th, 80–100th, 95–100th); the right column stratifies by the deployed clinical categories (low, average, high). The two columns are doing different jobs: the left shows that the underlying risk gradient is continuous and monotone, the right shows what survives after that continuum is collapsed into the three categories a report can print.
- **Reusable design:** Showing the continuous percentile stratification and the deployed category stratification side by side is the honest way to present a thresholded model. It lets a reader see exactly how much resolution the thresholds discard, instead of only seeing the categories the product ships with.
- **Boundary:** These are **cause-specific cumulative incidence** curves in MVP only — no PRACTICAL or All of Us curves are shown, so the absolute risks are US-veteran-specific and the paper's own Discussion says other systems "should examine model calibration in their own data before implementation". Incidence is observed, not predicted-versus-observed: **no calibration curve appears anywhere in the paper**, and calibration is what would license using these absolute numbers for counselling. Diagnosis is ascertained from ICD codes and the VA Central Cancer Registry, and metastasis from an NLP tool over VA records, so the curves inherit whatever detection pattern VA screening practice produces — a screening-intensity artefact and a true risk gradient are not separable here.
- **Locator:** [Paper: Fig. 3A–C]

## Table 1 — PHS601 alone, in MVP and PRACTICAL

- **Argumentative role:** Establishes that the polygenic component works, before family history and ancestry are added. It is the baseline against which Table 2 must be read.
- **Panel logic:** Rows are cohorts and ancestry strata — MVP overall and ancestry-stratified, then four PRACTICAL datasets (African ancestry n = 6,253, Asian ancestry n = 2,320, Cohort of Swedish Men n = 3,415, ProtecT n = 6,411). Columns are outcomes: any, metastatic and fatal prostate cancer in MVP; any, clinically significant and fatal in PRACTICAL. The cell is HR per standard deviation with 95% CI. In the overall MVP cohort: any **2.02** (1.97–2.07), metastatic **2.07** (1.95–2.17), fatal **1.96** (1.75–2.18).
- **Reusable design:** The caption states the exclusion rule up front — subsets with fewer than 50 events are omitted "given the unstable nature of the hazard ratio estimates". Publishing the suppression rule in the caption rather than in a footnote or not at all is what stops a reader from mistaking absent rows for untested ones.
- **Boundary:** The multi-ancestry claim holds only where the table is powered. Consistency is asserted for MVP strata with **more than 100 events**; the East Asian MVP subgroup was not statistically significant for metastatic or fatal disease, and the American subgroup's fatal-disease estimate was HR 2.22 with a CI of **0.98–4.25**, which crosses one. Directional consistency in an underpowered stratum is encouraging, not evidence. Note also that this is a table of **hazard ratios per standard deviation** — a within-cohort standardization, so a common HR across cohorts does not mean a common absolute risk, and nothing here speaks to calibration.
- **Locator:** [Paper: Table 1; Results, "Association of polygenic score with prostate cancer outcomes"]

## Table 2 — The full P-CARE model, in MVP and PRACTICAL

- **Argumentative role:** Nominally the paper's headline result. Read against Table 1, it is something more interesting: the demonstration that the extra components buy almost nothing at the population level.
- **Panel logic:** Same cohort rows as Table 1, with a fourth outcome column added (clinically significant prostate cancer). The caption restates the model composition — PHS601, first-degree family history, and genetic principal components. Beyond HR per standard deviation, the table carries the quintile-contrast statistics: across MVP and PRACTICAL, the lowest quintile runs HR **0.35–0.46** versus median and the highest quintile HR **2.48–4.03** across the four outcomes. The MVP overall HRs per s.d. are any **2.04**, metastatic **2.05**, fatal **1.95**.
- **Reusable design:** Reporting the nested model (Table 1) and the full model (Table 2) as two parallel tables with identical row and column structure lets a reader difference them by eye. Most papers would report only the full model, or a single incremental-fit statistic; this layout makes the comparison unavoidable — and to the authors' credit, the Discussion then states the null result plainly, that "family history and agnostic genetic ancestry have less prognostic value in the current multivariable model than in our prior model".
- **Boundary:** Table 2 versus Table 1 is 2.04 versus 2.02, 2.05 versus 2.07, 1.95 versus 1.96 — **differences well inside the confidence intervals**. The table therefore does not establish that P-CARE outperforms PHS601, and it contains no net reclassification, no decision-curve analysis and no likelihood-ratio test that would settle the question. The quintile spread of roughly six- to ten-fold between extremes is a discrimination statement, not a utility statement. The MVP estimates come from 10 iterations of 10-fold cross-validation *within* the development cohort, so the MVP column is internal validation and only the four PRACTICAL columns are external. And the family-history variable is not the same construct across cohorts: in MVP it is survey-reported **paternal** history, in PRACTICAL and All of Us it is any first-degree relative.
- **Locator:** [Paper: Table 2; Discussion]

## Table 3 — Characteristics of the deployed risk categories

- **Argumentative role:** The table that determines who actually gets screened differently. It converts the continuous model into the three-category output the clinical report prints, and then shows how the population falls across those categories.
- **Panel logic:** Rows are the three categories defined by thresholds of HR 0.75 and HR 1.5 **for metastatic prostate cancer**; columns describe the 585,418 MVP participants falling into each — the split is **25.1% low, 37.3% average, 37.6% high**. The clinically loaded rows are the cross-tabulations against known risk factors: **68.7%** of men with a positive family history land in high risk and only 5.6% in low risk; among participants self-reporting Black or African-American race, only **2.8%** are classified low risk.
- **Reusable design:** Anchoring the thresholds to metastatic disease rather than to any prostate cancer is the paper's sharpest design decision, and putting it in the table caption rather than burying it in Methods is what makes the categories interpretable. The cut points are also explicitly borrowed from routine clinical prediction tools in breast cancer, diabetes and cardiovascular disease, which is an argument from convention rather than from this disease.
- **Boundary:** HR 0.75 and HR 1.5 are **chosen, not derived** — no decision analysis, cost-effectiveness model or net-benefit calculation sets them, and no sensitivity analysis shows what happens if they move. Their consequences are severe: a "high-risk" tier containing **37.6% of the population** provides limited triage at the top end, and a category in which only 2.8% of Black participants can be placed on the low side means the model's individualizing benefit is distributed asymmetrically across groups. The table reports the distribution of categories against risk factors; it does not report outcomes within category by group, so it cannot show whether the within-group gradient predicts equally well where it is most compressed.
- **Locator:** [Paper: Table 3; Results, "Association of P-CARE with prostate cancer outcomes"]

## Table 4 — Cumulative incidence by risk category

- **Argumentative role:** Supplies the absolute numbers a clinician would say out loud. This is the tabular companion to Fig. 3's right-hand column, and the closest the paper comes to a counselling tool.
- **Panel logic:** Rows are the three P-CARE categories, columns are cause-specific cumulative incidence for any, metastatic and fatal prostate cancer at a given age. By age 80 the high-risk group reaches **37.4%** for any prostate cancer, **4.4%** metastatic and **0.8%** fatal, against **0.8%** metastatic in the low-risk group.
- **Reusable design:** Reporting all three endpoints in one table forces the reader to notice their very different magnitudes and therefore the very different stakes of a false positive at each. A 37.4% versus 0.8% contrast within the same row block is the strongest available argument for anchoring thresholds on metastasis rather than detection.
- **Boundary:** The metastatic contrast that justifies the whole screening intervention is **4.4% versus 0.8% by age 80** — a 5.5-fold relative difference over an absolute difference of 3.6 percentage points, and the fatal-disease incidence in the high-risk group is 0.8%, the same figure as metastatic incidence in the low-risk group. These are MVP estimates only, with no external cumulative incidence and no calibration evidence, so they are veteran-specific. The table gives no confidence bands in the caption and reports incidence in the presence of competing mortality only through the cause-specific formulation. The **risk-equivalent ages** derived from these curves — a high-risk man reaching the standard 55-year-old's detection risk at age 50, and the standard 70-year-old's metastatic risk at 63.5 — are not in this table; they sit in Supplemental Table 11, which was not opened directly.
- **Locator:** [Paper: Table 4; Results, "Association of P-CARE with prostate cancer outcomes"]

## Extended Data

Three Extended Data figures carry claims cited in the card, none of which were opened directly. Extended Data Fig. 1 re-cuts the Fig. 2 PPV analysis stratified by PSA level, which is where one would check whether the P-CARE enrichment is uniform across the PSA range or concentrated at the top of it. Extended Data Fig. 2 gives the percentage of ProtecT true-positive cases falling into high P-CARE categories — the sensitivity-side counterpart to Fig. 2's PPV, and the exhibit that would say how many clinically significant cancers a P-CARE-gated pathway would miss. Extended Data Fig. 3 carries the All of Us replication by category (74,331 samples: 4,473 cases, 69,858 controls), the source of the high-versus-average odds ratio of 2.41 (2.25–2.60) and the low-versus-average 0.48 (0.44–0.54), and the claim that these hold across the six All of Us genetic-ancestry groups. That last figure is the paper's only truly independent, non-VA, non-PRACTICAL validation, and it is reported as odds ratios from age-adjusted logistic regression on case-control status, not as time-to-event — so it replicates discrimination, not the hazard model.

## Cross-figure reading rule

Read Fig. 1 first, but read it as a claim about scope rather than about performance: it tells you which of the seven steps each later exhibit belongs to. Then go straight to **Table 1 and Table 2 together** — they are the paper's real result, and the only way to see the result is to difference them, because Table 2 alone reads as a headline and the pair reads as a null. Table 3 next, because it is where a continuous model becomes a clinical instruction and where the choice of HR 0.75 / 1.5 does all of the downstream work. Table 4 with Fig. 3 after that, since the curves and the age-80 numbers are the same evidence in two forms, and the categories in Fig. 3's right column are exactly Table 3's. Fig. 2 last: it is the smallest exhibit and the only one that shows the model changing what an existing test means, which makes it the best single answer to "why would a clinician order this" — and its overlapping confidence intervals are the best single answer to "why is there still a trial". [Paper: Fig. 1; Tables 1–4]
